# Microeconomics for healthy community business (Varian map)

A reading map for people who keep a LoopBiotek-style community solvent. It points at Hal R. Varian, *Intermediate Microeconomics: A Modern Approach* (8th ed.), by chapter title and number only. The notes under each title are this project's plain-language use of the ideas. They are not quotations from the book.

The full food rules — nutrition first, safety floors, export last, expansion only when failure probabilities stay under their thresholds — are in the root [README](../README.md) ([Nobody goes hungry](../README.md#nobody-goes-hungry), [How the Loop model works](../README.md#how-the-loop-model-works)). How many communities coordinate is in [Network of communities](../README.md#network-of-communities).

## Purpose

These chapters are the ones that help a community sustain a healthy business practice: decide what to spend, when to invest, how to treat risk, what may be sold, and how to live next to other sites without stripping its own floors.

Three Loop operations are the filter for every idea below.

- **Nutrition stack.** People, then breeders, then animal feed, then seed and propagation, then working stock and safety floors. Export is last.
- **Module 11 gates.** No growth step unless the chances of a nutrition shortfall, a seed-floor breach, an unserved energy deficit, and negative cash stay under their thresholds (the α limits).
- **Export-last.** A price, a buyer, or a bounty can act only on what those two rules have already released.

## High-yield spine

**Ch. 1 — The Market.** A community is optimizing under constraints, then looking for a balance that can persist. Pareto efficiency, in the textbook sense, means no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. Inside a cell that test is applied only after the nutrition stack. A trade that feeds a buyer by emptying a neighbor's plate fails the project's rule even if it looks efficient on a whiteboard.

**Ch. 2 — Budget Constraint.** Every week has a budget: feed, seed, hours, water, kilowatt-hours, and cash. Choosing one use means refusing another. That refused use is the opportunity cost. The habit is a ledger that shows the refusal, not only the purchase.

**Ch. 10 — Intertemporal Choice.** Money and goods now are not the same as money and goods later. Present value is how a capital purchase (CAPEX) is compared with the cash it is supposed to return in later weeks. A kit, a tank, or a roof that pays back only by skipping the nutrition stack or the α gates does not clear this chapter's test for the project.

**Ch. 12 — Uncertainty, and Ch. 13 — Risky Assets.** Outcomes are distributions: hatch, survival, yields, weather, prices. Risk aversion means a community prefers a smaller chance of a bad week over a glamorous average. Insurance and diversification are ways to pay, in advance, to keep one failure from ending the cell. This is the textbook neighbor of Module 11: expansion waits until those bad-week probabilities sit under α.

**Ch. 14 — Consumer's Surplus.** Benefit minus what was given up. Use it on a proposed purchase or a proposed sale. If the benefit is "revenue" and the cost is a thinner seed bin or a missed `12H` egg allowance, the surplus is not real for this project.

**Ch. 15 — Market Demand, and Ch. 16 — Equilibrium.** Buyers respond to price; that response is elasticity. A steep price on something residents must have (the community egg claim is the worked example: up to 12 eggs per person per week in the reference model) mostly transfers pain onto the people the stack is there to protect. Equilibrium is a resting point of supply and demand, not a moral permission to sell floors. Taxes and deadweight loss are a reminder that a clumsy fee on internal necessities destroys value. Fees, if any, belong on true external surplus.

**Ch. 18 — Technology, through Ch. 23 — Industry Supply.** These chapters cover how output scales with inputs, how to produce a given output at least cost, and how short-run decisions differ from long-run ones.

- Returns to scale: doubling the cell does not automatically double the food. Module 11 is the project's way of refusing a scale-up the probabilities do not support.
- Cost minimization: waste heat, purchased feed, and spare labor are costs. BSFL, earth shelter, and shared tools count when they actually cut those costs.
- Short run versus long run: this season's flock, tank, and seed are partly fixed. The long run is when capacity, land, and contracts can change.
- Sunk cost: money already spent on a bad tool is gone. It is not a reason to keep raiding the seed floor to "make the purchase worth it."
- Zero economic profit: in a competitive long run, revenue covers all costs, including the operator's next-best use of their time. "Accounting profit" can look fine while the operator is paying themselves nothing. Count that time.
- Rent: a payment above what an input needs in order to stay. A scarce plot, a working incubator, or a trusted brand can earn rent. Rent taken from residents' nutrition is the case the monopoly chapters warn against.

**Ch. 24 — Monopoly, and Ch. 25 — Monopoly Behavior.** A sole seller can hold price above cost and leave willing trades undone. Inside the community, residents are not that market. Do not extract from your own households: the nutrition stack already assigned them their claim. Market power, if it is ever used, belongs on external channels, and only after the stack has released a surplus. Price discrimination, bundling, and quantity games in those chapters are optional tactics for outside buyers. They are out of bounds against the people the cell feeds.

**Ch. 9 — Buying and Selling, and Ch. 26 — Factor Markets.** Households sell labor and buy goods. When wage jobs thin out, the labor-supply choice changes: more hours may go to the cell's own production because the outside wage is a weaker alternative. Factor markets are where wages, rent, and input prices are set. The community still pays for outside inputs it does not produce. It does not pretend labor inside the cell is free.

**Ch. 28 — Game Theory, Ch. 29 — Game Applications, and Ch. 30 — Behavioral Economics.** Several sites sharing a hub, a watershed, or a buyer are in a game. A one-shot prisoner's dilemma punishes the site that restrains itself while others do not. Repeated dealings make restraint easier to sustain, if cheating is visible and costly. Hold-up is what happens when one party invests and the other renegotiates once the investment is stuck. Fairness and present bias show up as real operator behavior: people reject offers that feel exploitative, and they overweight this week against a safer year. Write rules that assume those tendencies. The network section of the README is the project's use of these chapters.

**Ch. 31 — Exchange, Ch. 32 — Production, and Ch. 33 — Welfare.** Internal trade (eggs for labor, surplus greens for maintenance hours) can make both sides better off. Welfare here is not "whatever the market did." Equity is a separate question from Pareto efficiency. The nutrition stack is the project's equity floor: an exchange that is Pareto-improving only after someone misses meals is rejected before the efficiency talk starts.

**Ch. 34 — Externalities.** One site's waste, water withdrawal, or disease spillover lands on others. A commons (aquifer, shared road, local demand that can be flooded with identical surplus) needs a rule: caps, published withdrawals and exports, and a way to stop. The Coase idea — bargain when rights are clear and dealing is cheap — is a tool when the neighbors are few and the harm is measurable. It is a weak tool when the harmed party cannot bargain. Caps and published numbers are the default.

**Ch. 35 — Information Technology.** Tools and standards become more valuable as more sites use the same ones (complements and network externalities). The same success can become lock-in: a file format, a vendor, or a firmware line that the cell cannot leave without losing its history. Prefer versioned open standards (IFC and the schemas already in this repository) and keep an exit. A longer operations note is in [Network of communities](../README.md#network-of-communities).

**Ch. 36 — Public Goods.** A standard, a shared playbook, or a disease alert is costly to produce and cheap to copy. Free riding is the predictable result. Contribution has to be tied to something operators actually feel: certification, mutual-aid eligibility, or a vote on the next standard. Guilt is not a business practice.

**Ch. 37 — Asymmetric Information.** Sellers know more than buyers (lemons), and insured or contracted parties may take hidden risks (moral hazard). Incentives have to pay for what can be checked: nutrition-stack compliance, published floors, measured surplus. A badge that cannot be audited will be worn by sites that have not earned it.

### Secondary

**Ch. 11 — Asset Markets.** Some stocks are used up: a seed lot, a battery cycle, a soil amendment, a machine-hour. Depletable resources need an explicit drawdown rule so this year's cash does not spend the asset the next cohort needs.

**Ch. 17 — Auctions.** When plot time, incubator space, or machine hours are scarce inside the network, an auction can assign them to the use with the highest real bid. The bid is admissible only if the bidder's own nutrition stack and α gates still hold. An auction is not a way to buy someone else's floor.

## Practical checklist

| Varian idea | Chapter | Community habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Budget and opportunity cost | 2 | Keep a weekly ledger: feed, seed, hours, water, energy, cash. Write down what was refused. |
| Present value | 10 | Gate CAPEX on later cash without spending the nutrition stack. If payback needs the floors, do not buy. |
| Risk and insurance | 12–13 | Treat Module 11 α limits as the failure gates. Diversify species and buyers only on surplus. Do not "diversify" by eating the seed reserve. |
| Benefit minus cost | 14 | Score a purchase or a sale by what the cell gives up, including a thinner floor. |
| Elasticity | 15–16 | Test price changes on external surplus. Leave the reference `12H` egg allowance, and the rest of the nutrition stack, off the price experiment. |
| Returns to scale, sunk cost | 18–23 | Scale up only when α gates pass. Do not pour more into a sunk tool by raiding floors. Count operator time so "profit" is not unpaid labor. |
| Monopoly restraint | 24–25 | No extraction from residents. External channels only, and only after the stack. |
| Labor when wages thin | 9, 26 | Record on-site hours as a real factor cost. Outside wages are the alternative, not a reason to price local food above the community claim. |
| Repeated games, hold-up, fairness | 28–30 | Shared hubs run on visible rules, dues, and metered use. Contracts that invite renegotiation after one side has sunk cost get rewritten before the spend. |
| Exchange after a floor | 31–33 | Internal trade is welcome. Pareto talk starts after the nutrition stack, not before. |
| Commons | 34 | Publish withdrawals and exports. Respect watershed, grid, and local-market caps. |
| Lock-in | 35 | Versioned open standards. A vendor format that cannot be exported is a risk, not a feature. |
| Public goods and free riding | 36 | Ops data contributed to the commons is tied to certification or mutual aid. Meter shared chores. |
| Lemons and incentives | 37 | Certify what can be checked: stack compliance, floors, measured surplus. Unverified badges do not clear a bounty. |

## Explicit non-goals

- This file is not required reading for every operator. The operating rules in the README and the notebook are enough to run a cell.
- Hal R. Varian and the textbook do not endorse LoopBiotek. The chapter list is a map this project finds useful.
- Nothing here adds a research result. Planning values in the model are not verified commercial performance.
- Do not paste the book into the repository. Cite the title, the edition, and the chapter titles and numbers. There is no textbook PDF in this repo.
